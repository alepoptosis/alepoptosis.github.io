*Content warning: non-descriptive mentions of suicide.*

Animal Crossing has had a weirdly important place in my life.
Don't get me wrong, after the incredibly timed release of Animal Crossing: New Horizons kept millions of us somewhat sane during the pandemic, I am sure many people will harbour similar feelings, but my experience goes a bit little deeper and a little bit further back.

I was gifted a Nintendo DS Lite for my 10th birthday, a little over 20 years ago now. My parents didn't want me to get a console too soon for fear I'd stop playing outside - not that we could really "play outside" much where I am from but hey, their heart was in the right place. Animal Crossing: Wild World found me probably a year or two after that. I remember picking it off the shelf purely based on the cover art vibes, taking it home, and naming my village after the mythical land from the book I was in the process of writing (and which I continue using to this day whenever I am prompted to name a place).

I loved that game and played it religiously. The hunt for online guides soon followed, 12-year-old me wanting to learn how to obtain the elusive golden axe. The guide led me to a forum, the forum led me to my first love, T., who led me to meeting the people I would move to the Scotland with several years later, which finally led me to pretty much everything else that has happened in my life up until the moment I am typing this.
I know it's not that simple, but in some ways it kind of is. The thought exercise of imaging what my life would be like if I had never picked up that game is a frightening one. I do not entertain it often.

I bought a 2DS in 2017. I was incredibly late to the party with that one, everyone else having already moved on to the now ubiquitous (and highly controversial) Nintendo Switch. I did it simply to be able to play Animal Crossing: New Leaf. I was 21, the game had already been out for five whole years and the hype train wasn't even on the horizon anymore, but I hadn't experienced a new mainline Animal Crossing game since City Folk in 2009, and I was just done with exams, and god damn it I deserved it.

I loved that game and played it religiously. Being older and busier and better at games, I definitely played it for a shorter amount of time, probably just a year or so, squeezing all its content out directly into my throat, gulping it down so quickly I suspect I barely got to taste it.
So when I picked up my 2DS again last week, I had two cartridges waiting for me: New Leaf, from 2017, and Wild World, from 2007. Two perfectly preserved capsules of a time and a place I once lived in and that, unlike everything else in life, were still patiently waiting for me, wondering where I'd been.

New Leaf is fresher in my memory. The game music and sounds immediately take me back, my character has the signature blue hair I used to sport, and my house has the same layout I unconsciously follow in every single Animal Crossing game I have ever played. I am a girl, and unlike real life, I cannot change my gender here - I googled it. I run into Pecan, who is happy to see me and tells me I look a little older than the last time we spoke. I can see my reflection in the screen. It's been 9 years, and it feels like a lifetime ago. She asks me for a cherry, and I oblige before saving and quitting the game.

Wild World is slow and choppy and makes me smile immediately. Because my 2DS is set to English, the game is also in English, which is all wrong since it never was before, and the villagers still repeat the Italian affectations they adopted from me all those years ago. I am sure they are as happy to see me as the New Leaf villagers were, but I landed back in town on something called Yay Day (must be an English localisation thing) so no one acknowledges my absence - they simply ask for compliments. A villager I do not remember has moved out. Weeds are everywhere. I am also a girl here, but for some reason, it bothers me less. It's been 19 years, and my navigational muscle memory is stronger than in my hometown.

Earlier this year, in an effort to purge myself from this thought so that I may finally start to feel less nauseous, I scribbled down into a notebook: *I look back at the last six months of my life and I see ruins. The outline is there, recognisable, but it's not a place I can go back to. Me and the other survivors pick up the rubble and begin building again*. It rattled around my brain so long until it simplified and condensed and sharpened into a fine point, into a kind of mantra: *the past is a ruin I cannot live in*.

Every time I think about picking up one of those old Animal Crossing games and actually start playing again, I never follow through - not even for the most recent one, which I must have stopped playing only 4 or 5 years ago. They were fun and they were important and in some ways they saved my life, but the truth is painful yet simple: there is nothing left for me there. They are time capsules that I can do nothing more with than dust off, look at, and bury again. At the same time, I cannot bring myself to delete them. They sit like monuments I can visit from time to time to remember something gone, which is to say, they sit like gravestones. The past is a ruin I cannot live in.

I haven't spoken to T. in over 8 years. Six months ago my friend - who used to be his best friend back in high school - killed himself. Me and another friend were the ones to find him. After the funeral, I thought about reaching out to T., tell him the notebook he'd gifted him was still there, that he had been thinking about reconnecting in the months before he died, but in the end, I didn't. How do you begin a conversation with someone you used to not be able to go a day without talking to, after 3000 days of not talking? After what was said the last time we did? Knowing he has never even heard my voice since it changed? The past is a ruin I cannot live in.

Every point of my life I find myself living in feels like the one I could not possibly survive outside of. I look at my life now and I cannot imagine not having the people I have, not occupying the spaces I am in, not making the decisions I have made - but I am sure I would have said the same thing if you'd asked me 2 years ago and here I am now, having betrayed the very beliefs I thought I would hold forever and being glad I had the courage to, no matter how hard it was, no matter how much it hurt. I think about how life a year ago looked almost completely different, as it did 5 years ago, 9 years ago, 19 years ago, how it will continue to change beyond recognition and yet will continue to be mine. The past is a ruin I cannot live in.

Ruins are important. We visit them and we study them and we grieve them and we admire them for whatever is left, and we protect that whatever that's left, and we vow to learn from whatever created such destruction, never to be repeated again. They are and should remain destinations of pilgrimage and places of reflection and testaments to the thousands of lives each of us lives within their apparent single one. But ruins are also cold and still and unstable and incapable of sustaining life. 

I sat in my latest ruins for a bit before moving out. I will grieve them forever, like I do each ruin, like I do the person I was when I lived in them, and the people who lived there with me. Some ruins I can never return to. Others, thankfully, are tucked away, hidden in small plastic cartridges, waiting for me to come and visit.
